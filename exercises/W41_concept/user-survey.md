## Interview guide (used for all three)
**Intro (read out):** "Hi, I'm Adhurim from HSLU. I'm working on a project about how people experience AI in everyday life.
There are no right or wrong answers. I'm interested in your experiences, not your opinion on technology. About 20 minutes. Is it OK if I take notes?"

**Broad openers**
1. Tell me about the last time you came across AI in your everyday life. Where were you, what happened?
2. What do you use AI for in a normal week? Walk me through a typical situation.
3. Tell me about a moment when AI surprised you, positively or negatively.

**Deeper**
4. What happened the last time you weren't sure whether something was real or made by AI?
5. How has AI changed anything in your work or studies so far? What did that feel like?
6. What was the last news story about AI that stuck with you? What did you do or say afterwards?
7. Tell me about the last conversation you had about AI with friends or family.
8. What happened the last time you decided *not* to use AI for something?

**Success**
9. If you saw a website or exhibit about how people feel about AI, how would you notice afterwards that it was worth your time?
10. Where do you go when you want to know what "people in general" think about something? What happened the last time?

**Follow-ups:** "Can you tell me more?" · "What happened next?" · "How did that feel?" · (silence)
**Roles:** solo — I asked and took notes. Explain nothing, defend nothing, don't pitch the concept.

## Desk research: published surveys
Large US samples that back up (or qualify) what we heard. Note: all three are US data from 2024–2025, older than our dataset.

- **Pew, Sept 2025** (n = 5,023 US adults, 9–15 June 2025):
  - 50 % are more concerned than excited about AI (up from 37 % in 2021), 10 % more excited, **38 % equally both** → backs **S6** (mixed feelings).
  - **76 %** say it is extremely or very important to tell AI-made from human-made content, but **53 %** aren't confident they can → backs **S1**.
  - About 6 in 10 want more control over how AI is used in their lives → backs **S2**.
  - Under 30s are *more* pessimistic about creative thinking (61 % "worse") and relationships (58 %) than 65+ (about 4 in 10) → **qualifies S3**: young people feel more agency, but are not more optimistic about everything.
- **NORC, July 2025** (AmeriSpeak AI Adoption Report):
  - 51 % of Americans don't use AI at all for personal activities, only 14 % use it daily; 58 % of employed people never use it at work.
  - Nearly two-thirds of people 60+ report no personal use.
  - → Our primary group (weekly users, R6) is a **minority** of the US public. The secondary group (R7, rare users) is the majority, especially among older people. Don't assume the visitor uses AI.
- **Pew, Apr 2025** (n = 5,410 US adults + 1,013 AI experts, Aug–Oct 2024):
  - Public: 51 % more concerned than excited; experts: 15 %. Public 11 % more excited; experts 47 %.
  - **64 %** of the public expect fewer jobs over the next 20 years, and 56 % are extremely or very concerned about job loss → backs P2's job worry.
  - About 6 in 10 worry regulation will be too lax, and 62 % have little or no confidence the government will regulate AI effectively → backs **S2** (feeling powerless).
  - → The gap between the public and experts is a possible extra layer ("the people building it feel very differently") [A: not in our dataset; label as an outside source if used].

## Insights (hypothesis → result)
- **S1 · Real vs. fake is the shared experience (H1 ✅).** All three told a concrete story: P1 an Instagram video, P2 a photo of a public figure, P3 a video shared by someone he knows. Across all usage levels.
- **S2 · Acceptance and uncertainty, more than fear (H2 ✅ with nuance).** P1: "learn how to work with AI rather than ignore it"; P3: "I don't know enough about it to say what will happen". Nobody described themselves as scared.
- **S3 · Agency differs by how much people use AI (H3 changed).** P1 and P2 draw their own lines (personal reflection, unhappy client). P3 "didn't know how to check". *Not* shown by age in our interviews (no one 50+); the age difference comes from the survey data only.
- **S4 · Nobody reads surveys (H4 ✅).** Sources are Reddit, YouTube/Facebook comments, Instagram polls, LinkedIn, friends. P3: "I don't normally look for research studies."
- **S5 · Success = comparing yourself with others, explained simply (H5 ✅).** P1 "people my age", P2 "people in different professions", P3 "different age groups … simply, without too many technical words".
- **S6 · Mixed feelings (✅).** P1: time saved, but "learning less"; P2: faster, but "pressure to produce things faster".
- **S7 · NEW — representativeness is the value-add.** P2: "whether the people commenting represent the wider population or just a very vocal group". Our survey answers exactly that question.
- **S8 · NEW (desk research) — most people don't use AI much.** NORC: 51 % no personal use, nearly two-thirds of 60+ none. The piece must work for non-users and can't assume AI experience.

## What surprised us
- Nobody described themselves as "scared". The tone was practical: checking, adapting, drawing personal lines. → The excited ↔ concerned axis needs "none of these" as a full answer.
- P2 raised representativeness on her own (S7). → "1,083 representative US adults, not the loudest comments" becomes part of step 3.
- P1 wants to know how people *use* AI, P3 wants to learn *how to spot fakes*. Our dataset measures feelings and perceptions, not use or detection skills. → Say clearly what the data does and doesn't show.
- P2's "pressure to produce things faster" matches a survey item almost word for word (49 % of US adults see it). → Possible extra hook for working visitors.
